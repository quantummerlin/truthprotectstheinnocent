## What La Stampa Published

**La Stampa** (Saturday, 14 March 2026) ran a multi-story feature on the growing public debate around child welfare and alternative families in Italy. It covered two connected stories:

1. **A feature comparing the Trevallion family to the Pica family** — another Italian family who chose to homeschool their four children on a mountain farm, and were investigated by the same authorities (carabinieri, social workers, municipal officials), but whose children were never removed.

2. **The social workers' public response** to the visit by **Marina Terragni**, Italy's National Guarantor for the Rights of Children and Adolescents, who had visited the facility in Vasto 24 hours earlier.

3. **Threats against a juvenile court judge** at the L'Aquila children's court — where the security escort of the presiding president has been strengthened on orders from the Prefect.

## The Pica Family — The Case That Shows This Needn't Have Happened

La Stampa's feature story, reported from Accumoli (Rieti) by journalist Flavia Amabile, profiles the Pica family: a mother (Katia), a father (Guido), and four children who have been raised since 2004 on a mountain farm over a thousand metres high — two kilometres from any town, practising homeschooling, living with animals, growing their own food, and raising their children without school schedules, without antibiotics given casually, without shoes indoors.

Sound familiar?

The carabinieri came. The mayor came. The social workers came. They were puzzled. They asked why the children didn't wear shoes. Why their hair was tangled. Why their hands were dirty. Why they were with the animals. Why they weren't in school.

**The Pica family's answer: "We like it this way."**

And that was enough. No children were removed. The institutions investigated, asked questions, and accepted the family's right to live differently.

Today the Pica children are aged 17–22. They have no diplomas. They have no regrets. The youngest daughters, Sofia and Elisa, still go barefoot around the farm — and post photos to Instagram like everyone else their age.

### "Barefoot and Unschooled — We Sleep Near the Animals, But Here on the Mountain We Are Happy"
**By Flavia Amabile — La Stampa, 14 March 2026**
*Reporting from Accumoli (Rieti)*

They are not the "forest family." They live on a mountaintop over a thousand metres high — but they have much in common with the Trevallions. They are the Pica family: four children raised in the middle of nowhere, two kilometres from the nearest inhabited centre, a town called Accumoli in the province of Rieti. It has fewer than five hundred residents and a historic centre destroyed by the 2016 earthquake.

On this hilltop overlooking the epicentre of that quake, the Pica family arrived twenty-two years ago. A radical choice, made when Katia D'Apostolo and her husband Guido Pica decided to start a family. Katia, now 49, recalls: *"We lived in the town but only at night, because we had decided to build a house up here. Our first daughter was one year old; then the others arrived. We spent the whole day here, in a precarious situation. There was barely a gas canister leaning in a corner, two pans we washed under the outdoor tap, and no bathroom. We had the bare minimum — what we needed to be well."*

Being well meant being in nature. *"We wanted to live alone, peacefully, with our animals and our friends,"* continues Katia. *"We would never have gone to live in a city or a village."*

The bathroom arrived at the Accumoli mountain house when the first three children were already walking and the fourth was about to be born. By then there was also a proper kitchen, a dining room, bedrooms, a wood-burning stove. The Pica family moved to the mountains permanently, transforming the house into a farm: two hundred cows, about twenty pigs, around fifty chickens, two horses, some goats, dogs and cats. The four young Pica children grew up helping their parents sow vegetables, collect eggs, milk the cow, and make cheese. *"At first it was a game — then it became daily life,"* explains Katia.

And then there was school. During the early years they attended elementary school in Accumoli. *"At a certain point we decided to withdraw them, and it was the most beautiful choice I could have made,"* continues Katia. *"Full-time school had been introduced; there was an obligation to eat at school; during lessons there was too much competition and too many principles that didn't convince us. The idea of sending the children away early in the morning and not seeing them until late afternoon. The idea that they weren't really writing but just putting crosses on forms. And then not knowing what they eat — we make our own milk, our cheeses, our flour for bread and pasta. If we created this farm where we produce our own food, it's mainly for the children."*

From that day forward, she became the teacher. They *"learned from reality,"* she explains. *"That a hundred plus a hundred makes two hundred — they grasped it quickly while we were making a crostata together, for example. At first it was hard because we had to 'de-school.' We had to erase the idea that you learn for a good grade or to be liked by the teacher. You learn for yourself, for the desire to know."*

The institutions, meanwhile, did not stand aside. *"The mayor came to our house. The carabinieri. Social workers. They were puzzled, trying to understand. Like the Trevallions, we don't easily give a child an antibiotic; we don't go to hospital for check-ups; we wait for nature to take its course. Those who came asked about our lifestyle: why don't these children wear shoes? Why don't they comb their hair? Why do they have dirty hands? Why are they with the animals? Why aren't they in school? We answered: we like it this way."* And so the Pica children grew up. Today they are between seventeen and twenty-two years old. The youngest, Sofia and Elisa, still walk around the farm barefoot as they did as children. A primitive life? Yesterday, at the farm, a meteorology website was installing a webcam. And there isn't a local festival the Pica sisters don't attend — nor a photo that isn't on their Instagram profiles, just like their peers.

Any regrets about not going to school? *"None,"* assures Elisa. *"It was much nicer to wake up without an alarm, snuggle into mum and dad's big bed, and tell each other our dreams. Then the day began — with the cows, the chickens, and the rest."*

None of the Pica children has a diploma. They studied until sixteen — the age of compulsory education — then stopped. *"They don't need a diploma to work, and they have more than enough work,"* explains Katia. The first two have set up businesses of their own; the youngest help on the farm. *"And without us, you'd be lost now,"* said Elisa, giving her mother a big smile.

### "Threats to the Juvenile Court Judge in L'Aquila"
**La Stampa, 14 March 2026**

In one of the many custody hearings underway at the L'Aquila children's court, the parties and their legal representatives were present when a man burst in — shouting at honorary judge Chiara Gallo, threatening her with death: *"Who are you? You do as you please and tear children away from people."* And again, according to witnesses: *"I'll be back, and it'll be worse for you."* The attacker then fled before the carabinieri and police arrived — but the internal cameras had already captured everything and he appears to have already been identified.

This is the climate prevailing at the L'Aquila children's court, where, on the Prefect's orders, the escort protecting president **Cecilia Angrisano** — the judge at the centre of the "forest family" case — has been reinforced. She too has been targeted with serious threats, both direct and across social media platforms.

Social workers at the casa famiglia in Vasto — where the three Birmingham-Trevallion children are currently hosted — published a note through VastoWeb in response to criticism from the **National Guarantor for the Rights of Children and Adolescents, Marina Terragni**, who had visited the facility twenty-four hours earlier:

*"None of the operators of the Alto Vastese Social Service has training deficiencies — they have carried out their training seriously and continuously, possessing all appropriate tools for their profession."*

And further: *"After the mother was removed, the operators of the casa famiglia in Vasto restored good relations with the minors, making the reasons for the ordered transfer no longer applicable."*
